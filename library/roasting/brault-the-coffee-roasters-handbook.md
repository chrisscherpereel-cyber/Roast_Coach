## What it is

A single-author, practice-first manual aimed at the whole span from someone roasting 10 oz in a Behmor or an air popper up to a small commercial shop running a 10–20 lb drum machine; Brault repeatedly writes for both audiences in the same paragraph. Its scope is the full bean-to-cup chain — history, green sourcing and grading, equipment types, batch prep, the roast progression, then (in the missing Part III) cupping and brewing — with the roast itself occupying one chapter of seven. It rests almost entirely on the author's own commercial roasting and importing experience (he describes himself as an importer of Southeast Asian coffees, incl. Liberica/Excelsa, and reports his own pH measurements and his own blind taste tests); the only external authorities it leans on are the SCA green-coffee grading standard and a scattering of unnamed studies.

## Findings

### Colour names have no fixed temperature; the temperature is a per-machine calibration

- **Claim:** Roast-level names are colour targets, not temperature targets. The temperature that produces a given colour is a property of the machine + probe + bean, not of the roast level.
- **Mechanism:** Probe location and type change the reading; different machines distribute heat differently, skewing readouts high or low. The skew is systematic, so a profile transfers between machines by applying a constant offset.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion from his own practice. He gives no measurement series and names no study. He does state the practical rule (adjust by the same amount consistently).
- **Numbers:** 440°F on one machine ≈ 445°F on another for the same result; "lower your numbers by 5°F or more" when moving between machines. His own chart temperatures are stated to be based on "an industry-standard 10-pound roaster".
- **Page:** pp.PDF-92–93, 129–130.

### Roast level is defined by elapsed time past first crack, not only by temperature

- **Claim:** The operational definition of Medium / Full City / Dark is development time measured from audible first crack, with temperature as a secondary indicator.
- **Mechanism:** Development after first crack drives caramelisation; time at temperature, not peak temperature alone, determines how far butter/caramel/chocolate develop and how far fruit/floral/citrus recede.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion / stated industry convention. No study cited.
- **Numbers:** Medium = minimum ~60 s past audible first crack. Full City+ = ~2 min or more past first crack. First crack itself lasts ~1–2 min. Second crack must be sustained ≥30 s to count as entering Dark. Full second crack develops ~20–30 s after it begins. Vienna ≈ 2–3 min after second crack starts. Vienna → French ≈ 2–4 min after second crack. Post-second-crack "further development" period ≈ 1–2 min. His sample operations log records development times of 2.7 min and 4.0 min for two 10 lb batches.
- **Page:** pp.PDF-133–136, 144, 96.

### Second crack timing is not predictable from first crack

- **Claim:** The interval between first and second crack varies enormously between beans and cannot be assumed.
- **Mechanism:** Not explained beyond bean-to-bean variation; he notes second crack starts when the bean core reaches outer-bean temperature.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion from practice.
- **Numbers:** Some beans enter second crack within **1 minute** of the end of first crack even with ambient temperature lowered; others take **4–5 minutes**. If second crack lasts more than **3 minutes**, check you are not overshooting into too dark a colour.
- **Page:** p.PDF-144.

### Moisture loss and yield

- **Claim:** Roasting loses a predictable, roast-level-dependent fraction of mass, and the roaster must plan for it.
- **Mechanism:** Water driven off; density falls and porosity rises as the bean structure opens.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion; figures given as typical, with no measurement series.
- **Numbers:** ~13% mass loss at light roast; **17% or more** at darker roasts.
- **Page:** p.PDF-122.

### Density falls and porosity rises with roast, and this governs extraction

- **Claim:** Darker/slower roasts make a more porous bean that gives up more solids to the brew — hence more body — which is why espresso and cold brew want a slower roast.
- **Mechanism:** Increased porosity increases extraction of solids into the brew; slower roasting dries the bean more thoroughly.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion plus a physical demonstration he proposes (chew light- vs dark-roast beans). A refractometer is offered as the instrument that would measure it, but no refractometer data is presented.
- **Numbers:** Extend roast time by ~**20%** for espresso/cold brew and reduce heat to suit. Elsewhere stated as espresso should be roasted **10 to 20 percent slower**. (These two figures are in tension.)
- **Page:** pp.PDF-122, 117, 131, 97.

### Acidity: pH falls with darker roast, and Brault ties his own higher pH to slower roasting

- **Claim:** Lighter roasts brew more acidic; longer/darker roasting reduces acidity; his own slower roasts measure less acidic than commercially bought coffee.
- **Mechanism:** Chlorogenic acids decompose during roasting into caffeic and quinic acid; most coffee acids are reduced by heat, but some new acids form when coffee is significantly over-roasted, and he says these can be physically irritating.
- **Evidence:** Mixed. The chemistry (CGA decomposition) is standard textbook material stated without citation. The comparative pH figures are **the author's own informal measurements** of commercially purchased coffee versus his own, using a consumer pH meter; no protocol, sample size or replication given. Treat as author assertion, not a study.
- **Numbers:** Coffee pH range 4.3–5.6. Big-chain medium roasts he tested "most of the time" ≤4.9. His own coffees 5.2–5.6. He attributes the difference to **15–18 minute** roasts vs a "common industry practice of 12 minutes", plus better green sorting. Drinkers with acid sensitivity may tolerate pH ≥5.1. ~50% of original CGAs lost in a medium roast. CGA content 6–7% in Arabica, up to 10% in Robusta — but Robusta brews to a *lower* pH despite this. A Kenyan example is cited at pH 4.3, tasting like lemon juice. pH meter cost ~$30 or less (Milwaukee type named).
- **Page:** pp.PDF-122–124, 146.

### Citrus/acidity can be roasted out by controlling the rise rate in a specific window

- **Claim:** Excessive citrus in a bean (his example: Kenyan for espresso) can be reduced not just by roasting darker but by slowing the rate of rise in a defined temperature band.
- **Mechanism:** Not explained; asserted as an observed effect.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion from practice. This is one of the few genuinely actionable, non-obvious profiling levers in the book.
- **Numbers:** Slower roast with a lower temperature rise across the **410°F to 430°F** range.
- **Page:** p.PDF-146.

### Maillard is a specific, smellable window

- **Claim:** The Maillard reaction is perceptible as an aroma progression and occupies a defined temperature window.
- **Mechanism:** Amino acid + sugar browning reaction; aroma runs from yeasty/baking-bread to toasted bread.
- **Evidence:** Historical attribution to Louis Camille Maillard, "who first described this reaction in 1912" — the only named scientific attribution in the roasting chapter. The temperature window is Brault's own sensory observation.
- **Numbers:** Most prominent **350°F–390°F**; sometimes still perceptible up around **420°F**. Maillard described the reaction in **1912**.
- **Page:** pp.PDF-121–122.

### The roasting (S) curve: what it is, and what Brault does and does not do with it

- **Claim:** The roast curve is a plot of time against temperature (or colour); two probes are enough to work with; the curve is a recipe-recording device more than a control device.
- **Mechanism:** Bean-temperature probe gives the curve and doneness; ambient-air probe shows how much convective heat is being applied. Adjust air temp to hold the bean curve where you want it.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion / industry convention. The figure (p.PDF-126) labels the axes as °F vs Time and marks: **Air temp** and **Bean temp** as two traces; **Turning point (temp diff. max)**; **1st Crack**; **2nd Crack**; **End**; and two bracketed spans, **Development Time** (1st crack → 2nd crack) and **Second Development Time** (2nd crack → End). It also marks a **Δ°F** gap between the air and bean traces at the turning point. Notably, Brault's curve defines "Development Time" as first-crack-to-second-crack, which is *not* the common modern definition (first crack to drop).
- **Numbers:** Two probes stated to be sufficient. Turnaround ~160–185°F. RTD probes: accurate, durable, stainless-sheathed, expensive — required inside the bean mass. Thermocouples: less accurate, more fragile, cheaper — acceptable for ambient air only.
- **Page:** pp.PDF-92–95, 126–127.
- **What he does not do:** there is no rate-of-rise (RoR) concept anywhere in the book, no declining-RoR rule, no development-time-ratio (DTR) percentage, and no discussion of crash/flick. The curve is presented as a record to be annotated and reproduced, not as a signal to be shaped in real time.

### Fluid-bed roasters cannot be profiled the same way as drum roasters

- **Claim:** Bean temperature cannot be reliably measured in a fluid-bed roaster, so temperature control there is intrinsically less precise; the operator must substitute time-and-program control plus rigorous logging.
- **Mechanism:** Beans are suspended in the airstream, so no probe can sit in the bean mass; two or more air probes plus calculation are needed to infer bean temperature.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion.
- **Numbers:** "Two or more probes placed in different locations within the airflow." Reproduction is done by duplicating source temperature, bean weight and airflow.
- **Page:** p.PDF-95.

### Logging is the mechanism of reproducibility, and Brault specifies the fields

- **Claim:** Reproducible roasting is a logging discipline; logs transfer between operators on similar machines.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion. The value here is the field list, which is a concrete schema.
- **Numbers / schema:** His "Sample Operations Log" columns are: **Bean, Weight, Time, Turn-around Temp, Turn-around Time, Temp/Differential at First Crack, Development Time, Development Air Temp, Development Airflow, Second Crack, Dump Temp.** Two worked rows: *Jampit*, 10 lb, 12 min, turnaround 170°F at 1.0 min, first crack 387°F with an 80°F differential, development 2.7 min, development air temp 455°F, airflow 7, second crack 442°F, dump 445°F. *D. Bourbon*, 10 lb, 14 min, turnaround 165°F at 1.1 min, first crack 392°F/70°F differential, development 4 min, development air 445°F, airflow 7, second crack 440°F, dump 443°F. A separate, coarser "Sample Log" for small-machine operators uses: **Name, Species, Crop, Date, Roast Level, Roast Type, Time, Notes.**
- **Page:** pp.PDF-96, 167–168 (the second log's body is in the missing Part III but its column headers survive).
- **Worth noting:** the "Temp/Differential at First Crack" field — bean temp plus the air-to-bean gap — is the only place the book treats the air/bean differential as a logged number rather than a picture.

### Airflow is a staged control, and it must be traded against heat

- **Claim:** Airflow is stepped up through the roast, and raising it requires lowering the flame.
- **Mechanism:** Increased airflow vents smoke and lengthens the development stage; without a matching heat reduction it overheats the roaster.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion / common drum-roaster practice.
- **Numbers:** On a 1–10 scale: start at **3**, move to **7** at the development phase, **10** for cooldown between roasts. Safety cutoff should typically be set to **500°F or below**. Most drum roasters run ambient between **430°F and 480°F** after warm-up.
- **Page:** pp.PDF-115–116, 144.

### Batch size interacts with roast level

- **Claim:** A roaster's usable batch size is smaller for dark roasts than for light, and stretching a dark roast over too long a curve flattens the cup.
- **Mechanism:** Darker roasts take longer to develop; beyond a weight threshold the cascading action of the bean mass changes and roast quality deteriorates.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion from practice.
- **Numbers:** Example given — 12 oz of screen-16 beans for a medium roast but only **8–10 oz** for a dark roast on the same machine. An 8 lb-rated drum may run 9–10 lb well before quality falls off. Explicit failure mode: "If you stretch your roasting curve too long for a dark roast, the taste profile will be flat and bland."
- **Page:** p.PDF-114.

### Roast times by equipment class

- **Claim:** Roast duration is a property of the machine class more than of intent.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion, with one named anecdote.
- **Numbers:** Drum roasters typically **10–18 min**. Behmor averages **18–20 min for 10 oz**. Large commercial drums can do **20 lb in 12 min**. Commercial fluid-bed: very hot air, short times (no figure given). A named counter-example: a Costa Rican farmer's **24-minute** slow "chocolate roast" for Arabica catuai, which Brault reports as excellent despite most American roasters expecting a "baked profile" from it.
- **Page:** pp.PDF-116–117.

### Charge conditions shift the whole roast

- **Claim:** Green bean starting temperature and humidity change roast timing enough to require compensation.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion.
- **Numbers:** Worked example — beans normally stored at 68°F versus beans carried three hours in a car at 10°F will need a timing adjustment. Beans left in humid summer air roast more slowly. In cold weather he raises the flame ~**5 percent**.
- **Page:** pp.PDF-118, 96.

### Cooling is part of the roast

- **Claim:** Beans continue roasting exothermically after the drop, so the drop point must anticipate the carry-over, and faster cooling is better.
- **Mechanism:** Residual bean heat; colour continues to develop during cooling.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion; he explicitly flags that "there is a running debate" about how much cooling time affects taste and that faster is only "generally agreed" to be better.
- **Numbers:** Two inconsistent thresholds are given — get below **200°F** as fast as possible (p.PDF-137) and get below **~170°F** within a few minutes (p.PDF-144). Ideal cool-to-touch time **3–8 min**; his own drum roaster achieves **3–4 min**; his 20 lb grill roaster **6–8 min** for 20 lb. The Behmor's cooling is fixed at **13 minutes** with no user control. Practical rule: "pull the beans just as they are entering the color stage you want" and expect further development during cooling.
- **Page:** pp.PDF-132, 137–138, 144.

### Some species and origins do not follow the first-crack / second-crack script at all

- **Claim:** The crack-based roast model is Arabica-shaped and breaks for other species.
- **Mechanism:** Not explained; presented as observed behaviour.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion, but grounded in his stated specialism (he imports Robusta, Liberica and Excelsa).
- **Numbers / specifics:** Robusta often has an **almost silent first crack** and can go from second crack to burnt within **two minutes**. Peaberries are ~**40% greater in mass and density** than split beans, often have **no audible first crack**, and need their own profile — "always start from scratch when trying to profile peaberries", standard S curves for non-peaberries are inaccurate for them. Tiny beans (as small as **8-screen**) roast faster and need a reduced flame. Ungraded/mixed-size beans should be taken to a finish temperature at least **5°F higher** than graded beans to bring the slow-roasting fraction into range. Browning aroma progression (hay/bread/toast) is stated to be typical of Arabica and may not hold for Robusta.
- **Page:** pp.PDF-117–119, 132, 146.

### Recalibrate every crop and season

- **Claim:** No two lots or seasons of the same bean roast the same or share the same optimal roast level.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion, with his own product as the example.
- **Numbers:** For his Liberica, the finish temperature varies **435°F to 455°F** depending on beans and season — a 20°F swing on the same nominal product.
- **Page:** p.PDF-118.

### Green-bean grading: the SCA classification

- **Claim:** Green lots are graded by counted defects per fixed sample mass, and the grade sets the price.
- **Evidence:** **Institutional standard**, attributed to the SCA (Specialty Coffee Association; Brault notes it was formerly SCAA). This is the strongest external authority in the book. Note that Brault states the sample size **twice and inconsistently**: "fewer than three full defects per 350 grams" on p.PDF-103 versus the SCA's 300 g basis used throughout pp.PDF-110–111. The 300 g figure matches the actual standard; treat the 350 g line as an error.
- **Numbers:** Sample = 300 g properly hulled coffee. **Specialty Grade (1):** ≤5 full defects, no primary defects, ≤5% over/under stated screen size, must have ≥1 distinctive attribute in body/flavor/aroma/acidity, free of faults and taints, **no quakers**, moisture 9–13%. **Premium (2):** ≤8 full defects, primary defects permitted, ≤3 quakers, moisture 9–13%. **Exchange (3):** 9–23 full defects, ≥50% by weight above screen 15 with ≤5% below screen 14, no cup faults, ≤5 quakers, moisture 9–13%. **Below Standard (4):** 24–86 defects. **Off Grade (5):** >86 defects. "Full defects" = black beans, moldy beans, significant foreign objects, fruit/leaves/sticks. "Primary defects" = parchment/husks, broken/chipped beans, insect damage, floaters/quakers, small stones, tiny objects.
- **Page:** pp.PDF-103, 110–111.
- **Brault's own anecdotal survey:** he poured out supermarket beans from four sources and reports a top-selling US branded blend at **>20% defects** (below Exchange Grade) and the world's top-selling brand's most expensive whole-bean espresso line at **>15% defects**, versus a national donut-shop retailer's clean lot. **Evidence: author's own uncontrolled inspection, four samples, brands unnamed, method unstated.** Do not treat these percentages as data.

### Composition tables (green vs medium roast)

- **Evidence:** Presented without attribution; consistent with standard reference figures, so effectively textbook convention restated. All values approximate.
- **Numbers:**

| Component | Green | Medium roast |
|---|---|---|
| Cellulose fiber | ~33% | ~33% |
| Oils/fats | ~13% | ~13% |
| Protein (aminos) | ~11% | ~9% |
| Sugars | ~8% | ~4% |
| Chlorogenic acid | ~7% | ~2% |

- **Page:** pp.PDF-57–58. The roast-driven halving of sugars and the ~3.5× reduction in chlorogenic acid are the two numbers that matter for profiling.

### Antioxidants decline with roast

- **Claim:** Antioxidant content is highest in lighter roasts and is destroyed as the roast progresses; they are only bioavailable after heating.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion. No study named; the "activated ... after subjected to at least 170°F" claim in particular is unsupported here.
- **Numbers:** 200–550 mg per six-ounce cup in a medium roast; activation threshold stated as ≥170°F.
- **Page:** p.PDF-125.

### Processing method sets the flavour baseline the roast then works with

- **Claim:** Wet/dry/honey processing determines a bean's flavour direction before the roaster touches it.
- **Mechanism:** Time in contact with pulp and skin lets sugars and nutrients migrate into the seed.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion / industry convention. Consistent with, but far less detailed than, the Kornman fermentation ebook — see the companion extract.
- **Numbers / mappings:** Dry/natural → fruitiness, brown sugar and honey notes, stronger fuller profile. Washed → nutty, spiced, chocolate tones. Honey/pulped natural → more developed profile with fruit and caramel notes. Wet-hulled (e.g. Sumatra) → rich, earthy; parchment removed at **20–24% moisture**, then dried to **~12%**; produces a dark green, almost bluish bean often with tip cracking, but only if sun-dried outdoors — wet-hulled coffee dried indoors in heated drums lacks these notes. Target drying moisture ~11%. Kiln-dried beans stated to have less-developed flavour than sun-dried.
- **Page:** pp.PDF-46–49.

### Taste preference is physiologically split, which bears on blending

- **Claim:** Drinkers divide into front-palate (acidity/aroma/sweetness → Arabica) and back-palate (body/bitterness → Robusta and hybrids) types, and this is stable per person.
- **Evidence:** Two strands, of very different quality. (a) A **University of Florida study** is named — though not cited to a paper — for the population split into taste-impaired / normal / supertaster; he also refers vaguely to "several studies performed and published by institutions in the United States, Canada, and Germany". (b) The coffee-specific back-palate/front-palate mapping and the 50/50 preference split rest on **the author's own "more than 10,000 blind taste tests"**, which are unpublished, unprotocolled and unverifiable.
- **Numbers:** 15% taste-impaired, 60% normal tasters, 25% supertasters. Taste bud counts can differ 5× between two people, in rare cases approaching 100×. Roughly half his testers preferred Arabica, half Robusta. Suggested competition blend: Arabica base + 20% Robusta + a touch of Excelsa. He also notes SCA cupping criteria were designed to evaluate Arabica, so the scoring system is structurally biased toward front-palate coffee.
- **Page:** pp.PDF-54–55.

### Flavour faults are often the bean's fault, not the roast's

- **Claim:** Objectionable notes — sour lemon, musty, licorice, metallic — frequently originate in the green and its processing, not in roasting error.
- **Mechanism:** Not explained.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion, but he supplies a genuinely useful diagnostic protocol: run the same roast process on a different origin; if the fault does not reproduce, blame the bean.
- **Page:** pp.PDF-146–147.

## Roast defects

Brault's defect material splits cleanly into two groups that a diagnostics dictionary should keep separate: **green-bean defects** (Chapter 4, the bulk of it, sorted before or after roasting) and **roast-stage faults** (scattered through Chapter 5 and elsewhere, caused by the roaster). He does not use the modern roast-defect vocabulary — **"scorching", "tipping", "facing" and "underdevelopment" do not appear as named defects anywhere in this file**, and "baked" appears only once, in passing.

### Green-bean defects (Ch. 4, pp.PDF-102–111)

| Defect | What it looks like | Cause | Stage | Brault's verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Broken beans / "ears"** | Bean fragments; an "ear" is a bean with the middle broken out of the outer part | Mechanical damage in processing/milling | Pre-roast (green) | Cosmetic only, provided it is not also mouldy or eroded. Counts as an SCA *primary* defect |
| **Quakers** | Very light-coloured beans that fail to darken with the rest of the batch | Unripe or underdeveloped cherries picked with the ripe | Detectable only **after** roasting — he says it is hard to identify a quaker in the green, so remove post-roast | Usually cosmetic. Diagnostic he recommends: pick out a handful, grind and brew them alone to find out whether they will actually hurt the cup. Zero quakers permitted in SCA Specialty Grade; ≤3 in Premium; ≤5 in Exchange |
| **Bug bites / insect damage** | Holes bored through the bean; tunnels may trap debris and grow green mould | Common coffee beetle burrowing through beans in the ripe fruit | Pre-harvest / in-cherry | "Normal to find a few in every batch." The mould is probably not harmful but **remove any bean with holes** |
| **Dents** | Rounded marks on the bean surface | Bird beaks pecking the cherries; also processing dings, which look similar | Pre-harvest or processing | Usually no impact on quality |
| **Moldy beans (individual)** | Discolouration, especially along a broken edge | Improper drying or storage above the moisture standard | Post-harvest storage | Remove the individual bean. He argues significant mould is rare in properly dried and stored coffee (standard: **<12% moisture in the bean**) and claims most tests show less mould on coffee than on tomatoes or strawberries — **asserted, no test named** |
| **Mold (lot-wide surface bloom)** | Obvious powdery whitish surface growth; strong musty unpleasant odour | Storage in wet conditions | Storage | **Discard the entire lot.** He states this never appears as isolated beans — if present, the whole lot has it |
| **Chaffy beans / silverskin** | Heavy chaff, especially in the fold of the bean | Normal; much heavier on dry-processed than on washed coffee | Green, carried into the roast | **Not a defect** for health or taste — but it is a **fire hazard**. Sieve or colander the beans before roasting if chaff is heavy |
| **Black beans, foreign objects, fruit/leaves/sticks, small stones** | — | Field and mill contamination | Green | SCA "full defects" (black, mouldy, significant foreign objects, fruit/leaves/sticks) and "primary defects" (parchment/husks, broken/chipped, insect damage, floaters/quakers, small stones, tiny objects) |
| **Not a defect — "monsooned"** | Deliberate controlled molding/fermenting of beans, analogous to cheese-ageing | Intentional process, historically an artefact of long sea transport | Post-harvest | He flags this explicitly so it is not mistaken for spoilage: if a bean was monsooned, it will say so in its advertising |
| **Not a defect — honey-process staining** | Pulp stains on the bean | Normal to honey processing | Processing | Expected, not a fault |
| **Not a defect — colour variance** | Bean colour ranging from yellowish cream through grey-green to dark brown; reddish or yellowish dried-pulp coating | Species and processing; he cites naturally dried Excelsa appearing both reddish and dark green in the same lot ("Christmas Tree Beans") | Green | "Color is never really a fully reliable measure of bean quality." Naturally dried and high-altitude beans tend darker green, indicating density and higher chlorophyll |

### Roast-stage faults (Ch. 5 and elsewhere)

| Fault | How you detect it | Cause Brault gives | Stage | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Uneven roast / outside darker than inside** | Take a whole bean, a broken bean piece, and grounds from the same lot and lay all three on the same colour swatch — they should all match the same square. If the outside is darker than the inside, the profile is wrong | Roast time too short, **or** an uneven temperature-rise curve | Whole roast | p.PDF-137 |
| **Flat / bland "stretched" dark roast** | Cup is flat and bland | Dark-roast curve stretched too long for the batch size; fix is a smaller batch weight for dark roasts | Development onward | p.PDF-114 |
| **"Baked profile"** | Named only as what most American roasters *expect* from a slow roast — a poor temperature-rise gradient. Brault does not define its sensory signature, and immediately offers a counter-example (a successful 24-min roast) | Poor temperature-rise gradient over a long roast | Development | p.PDF-117 |
| **Over-roasting into irritating acids** | Not sensory — he frames it as a physiological effect on the drinker | Acids that form when coffee is significantly over-roasted, which "often can have an irritating effect on our bodies" | Past second crack | p.PDF-123 |
| **Loss of origin character (roast dominating bean)** | The cup tastes of the roast rather than of the coffee; all beans converge on the same profile | Dark roasting and scorching. He quantifies it: ~**70% of the original profile taste** lost by French; at Italian/Spanish "almost all of the origin characteristics are lost" and smoke completely dominates | Vienna → French → Italian | pp.PDF-135–136 |
| **Runaway second crack into burnt** | Second crack running long | Some beans (Robusta particularly) go from second crack to burnt within **2 minutes**; if second crack lasts more than 3 minutes, check for overshoot | Second crack | pp.PDF-144, 146 |
| **Bean fire / chaff fire** | Ignition in the drum or on the cooling tray — beans may even ignite as they are poured out, from the oxygen blast | Flaming chaff contacting oily dark-roast beans. Chaff is highly flammable and becomes sticky on oily dark roasts. He states this is the origin of *most* roaster fires | Italian/Spanish level; also cooling/dumping | pp.PDF-136–138 |
| **Cold-charge / humid-green timing drift** | Roast runs off its expected timing | Green beans charged at unusually low temperature, or beans that have absorbed humidity | Charge | p.PDF-118 |
| **Green faults masquerading as roast faults** | Overwhelming sour lemon, musty, licorice, or metallic notes | Often the bean and its processing, not the roasting. **Diagnostic:** run the same process on a different origin; if the fault does not reproduce, blame the bean | Cupping | pp.PDF-146–147 |

## What it does not establish

1. **Nothing about cupping, resting/degassing, storage, brewing, or blending.** All of Part III and the closing artisan section are present in this file as headings with blank bodies (pp.PDF-150–195). Section titles survive — "The Importance of Resting", "FRESH OFF THE ROAST", "THE THIRD DAY", "THE EIGHTEENTH DAY", "MULTI-TEMPERATURE ROAST BLENDING", the SCA cupping attribute list (fragrance, aroma, flavor, aftertaste, acidity, body, balance, sweetness, uniformity, cleanliness) — but their content does not. Any claim attributed to Brault about resting curves or degassing days cannot be sourced from this copy.

2. **No rate-of-rise, no development time ratio, no crash/flick.** The modern quantitative profiling vocabulary is entirely absent. The S-curve figure has no RoR trace; there is no percentage-of-total-time development target; and he defines "Development Time" as first-crack-to-second-crack rather than first-crack-to-drop, which conflicts with prevailing usage. If your app computes DTR or RoR, Brault is not a source for it.

3. **Its own temperature tables contradict each other.** First crack is put at "approaching 390°F" (p.PDF-132), at 380–395°F (p.PDF-139), and at 380–405°F (p.PDF-143). Second crack is 440–450°F in one table and 445–465°F in another. Full City is ~435–445°F in the prose and 450°F on the chart. Cooling threshold is 200°F in one place and ~170°F in another. Espresso slow-down is "about 20 percent" in one place and "10 to 20 percent" in another. None of these are reconciled.

4. **"Arabic" roast level is not in the book.** Nor are Scandinavian, Half City, Northern Italian, or any of the other names that circulate. His chart is explicitly his own seven-name invention, chosen for recognisability, and he says so.

5. **New England and American are not defined levels here.** They appear in one clause as alternative names some roasters use for the light end of his Light Roast band. No temperature, no colour swatch, no distinction drawn between the two.

6. **Almost nothing is externally cited.** Across the roasting chapters the only named external references are Maillard (1912, historical attribution), the SCA green-coffee grading standard, and an unnamed University of Florida taste-bud study. Every roast temperature, every timing, every flavour mapping, the pH comparison, the supermarket defect survey, and the 10,000 blind taste tests are the author's own unpublished assertions. There is no bibliography of studies; the Resources section is a species list and vendor pointers.

7. **No sensory lexicon and no measurement of colour.** Roast level is matched by eye against a printed swatch he made. There is no Agtron/colorimeter number anywhere, no reference to SCA roast colour classification numbers, and no way to translate his chart into an instrument reading.

8. **No moisture, density or weight-loss measurement protocol.** The 13%/17% moisture-loss figures are given as typicals with no method for measuring them on your own roasts, and no discussion of using weight loss as a development proxy.

9. **Bean-temperature probes are treated as arbitrary.** He is right that probes differ, but he offers no calibration procedure beyond "adjust by the same amount consistently" — no reference bean, no ice-point check, no cross-machine calibration method.

10. **Single-author scope.** There is no comparison of competing profiling philosophies, no engagement with the roasting literature (Rao, Schenker, Hoos, the SCA roasting curriculum), and no data. It is a competent, honest, experience-based handbook that repeatedly and explicitly tells the reader its numbers are guidelines — which is the right posture, but it means it cannot be used as an authority for any specific number.
