# Roasting Foundation — CoffeeMind
Morten Münchow. *Roasting Foundation*, 2nd edition, 2018. CoffeeMind Press, Hanssstedvej 35, DK-2500 Valby, Denmark. ISBN 978-87-93507-03-6. Graphic design Mark Gry Christiansen; printed by LaserTryk.dk. © CoffeeMind Press (p. 71). 72 pages; Part I is the roasting curriculum (pp. 7–48), Part II is coffee-business material outside the curriculum (pp. 49–67), followed by a resources list (pp. 68–69).

## What it is
It is the foundation-level handbook for people deciding whether coffee roasting is for them and preparing for the SCA's Roasting Foundation exam — beginners, not production roasters (pp. 6–7, 48). It teaches a deliberately simplified model of the roast: the stages from green to brown, heat transfer, time, the roast profile as an outcome of a flame-and-airflow plan, numeric rules of thumb, roast logging, and roastery fire safety. Its author is unusual among roasting writers in holding an academic post — he has collaborated with the University of Copenhagen's Food Science Faculty since 2003, generated 20+ coffee research projects of which five are scientifically published, and chairs education and research in the Coffee Roasters Guild (pp. 4–5) — but the handbook itself rests overwhelmingly on teaching convention and personal consulting experience, and cites *one* peer-reviewed paper: Giacalone & Münchow et al. 2018, invoked twice as the empirical backbone of the whole "colour is the dominant variable" argument (pp. 14, 48, 68). Everything else numeric in it is presented explicitly as a rule of thumb, not a finding.

## Definitions it fixes

- **Development / development time** — traditionally, the interval from the start of cracking until the roast is terminated (p. 9); equivalently the part of the roast after 1st crack (p. 13). The book flags this as a *convention, not a physical fact*: the bean is developing from the moment it is dropped into the roaster (p. 9), and arguably keeps developing until it reaches final ambient temperature in the cooling tray (p. 13). The convention is defended on the grounds that aroma development accelerates sharply after 1st crack as the beans brown, so that stretch deserves particular attention (pp. 13, 14).
- **Roast degree** — the darkness of the coffee, set by the temperature the beans reach at the end of the roast: higher end temperature = darker colour (p. 13). Named "by far the main factor of the roasting process that has the biggest influence on the flavour" (p. 13).
- **Temperature midway point (CoffeeMind's own teaching definition of a 'technical medium roast')** — the bean temperature exactly halfway between the start of 1st crack and the start of 2nd crack, computed as `(Temp2nd − Temp1st) / 2 + Temp1st` (pp. 16–17). Devised so that roast degrees are comparable across different roasters in different training centres.
- **1st crack** — caused by water vaporising inside the bean until steam pressure exceeds what the bean structure can bear and a microscopic crack forms (p. 13). Defined operationally not by the first single pop but by the point where the whole batch is "rolling" (p. 14).
- **2nd crack** — caused by roast gases (chiefly CO₂) generated during the brown phase (p. 13).
- **Endothermic vs exothermic phase** — endothermic = the reaction absorbs energy; exothermic = it releases it. Coffee is endothermic while green and moist and turns exothermic past 1st crack, so after 1st crack the system has two heat sources: the roaster's and the bean's own (p. 33).
- **Heat vs temperature** — heat is transferred and absorbed *energy*; temperature is a measure of the *amount* of absorbed heat energy in the material (p. 21). The book's metaphor: flame = accelerator, temperature = speedometer (p. 22).
- **Flash roast** — total roast time under three minutes (p. 27).
- **Roast plan vs roast profile** — the plan is the flame/airflow control sequence (the *cause*); the logged temperature-over-time curve is the *result*/effect (p. 39).
- **Percentage change** — `((Y − X) / X) × 100%`, used for both roast loss (negative) and volume gain (positive) (p. 12).

## Findings

### Roast colour dominates sensory outcome; timing is secondary
- **Claim:** In a roast design that varies both colour and time, final colour accounts for the large majority of sensory variation; the timing dimension accounts for only a small fraction.
- **Mechanism:** Not mechanistically explained; presented as an empirical result from a quantitative descriptive sensory evaluation of profiles that modulated colour and time independently.
- **Evidence:** Peer-reviewed — Giacalone & Münchow et al. 2018 (the author is a co-author).
- **Numbers:** Colour modulated ~80% of sensory variation; timing only ~20%.
- **Page:** 14, restated in the conclusion (p. 48) as the reason colour is "the most important parameter to keep track of" from a quality-control perspective.

### Acidity falls and bitterness rises with roast degree
- **Claim:** As a general rule, increasing roast degree increases bitterness and decreases acidity; light roasts can be very acidic, dark roasts are more bitter.
- **Mechanism:** Not given — asserted as a general rule and drawn as two crossing straight lines against roast degree.
- **Evidence:** Teaching convention / assertion (Fig. 5).
- **Numbers:** None; purely qualitative and monotonic.
- **Page:** 14–15.

### There is no right roast degree, only a target customer segment
- **Claim:** Roast degree should not be framed as right or wrong; the correct target is the preference of the intended customer segment, established on the cupping table and preferably by consumer studies, then replicated via bean temperature.
- **Mechanism:** Market preference, not chemistry. Cited evidence of drift: a decade ago an espresso roasted to the start of 2nd crack counted as a Scandinavian light roast; today the same coffee reads as dark in Scandinavia and the UK.
- **Evidence:** Assertion from consulting/teaching experience.
- **Numbers:** "past ten years" of market movement toward lighter roasting.
- **Page:** 15.

### Bean temperature maps to colour only *within* a profile style
- **Claim:** Bean temperature correlates with roast colour, but the correlation is specific to a given profile; significantly different profiles have different bean-temp-to-colour relationships. A slower roast reaches a given colour at a *lower* bean temperature; a fast roast must end hotter to match the same colour.
- **Mechanism:** Heat-induced reactions depend on time-at-temperature, not temperature alone, so time contributes colour independently of peak temperature.
- **Evidence:** Physical-chemical reasoning stated as fact; no study cited.
- **Numbers:** None. Additionally flagged: seasonal variation in ambient temperature and relative humidity shifts the bean-temp/colour relationship (p. 13).
- **Page:** 13, 27.

### Bean temperature readings are not comparable between machines
- **Claim:** Bean temperature measurements vary significantly between machines; comparisons of temperature values between different roasters can rarely be used — a probe reading is only meaningful as a consistent value on the same machine.
- **Mechanism:** Different positions in the drum have different temperatures; probe thickness causes heat delay; probe length and width change both the absolute value and the rate of change (a short probe can be chilled by the roaster cabinet).
- **Evidence:** Assertion / engineering reasoning.
- **Numbers:** None given, but the practical implication is stated elsewhere: end temperature "can be any value between 185 °C and up to 225 °C" partly *because* probe installation differs by brand (p. 36).
- **Page:** 21.

### Energy input should be planned high-to-low, and reduced before 1st crack
- **Claim:** Speed of energy input should go from high to low across the roast, with the flame reduced several times *before* 1st crack, and reduced again around 1st crack.
- **Mechanism:** Three stacked reasons — (1) early beans are moist and dense and tolerate high energy without surface damage; (2) later beans are fragile and conduct heat poorly from surface to centre, so high input scorches; (3) past 1st crack the exothermic reaction adds a second, growing heat source, so external heat must come down or the roast runs away.
- **Evidence:** Teaching convention plus thermodynamic reasoning; explicitly qualified — "there are no universal truths but these should be considered as guidelines."
- **Numbers:** None attached to the principle; see rules of thumb below.
- **Page:** 32, 33, 39.

### Rules of thumb for a first profile
- **Claim:** A usable starting profile for an unfamiliar coffee/roaster.
- **Mechanism:** n/a — calibration heuristics, explicitly analogised to espresso's "25-second rule": enough to point you in the right direction on a first attempt, never enough to win a competition.
- **Evidence:** Teaching convention, stated as such.
- **Numbers:** Inlet (smoke) temperature 200 °C, range 170–230 °C. Time to 1st pop 9 minutes, range 5–13. Reduce flame after 1st pop. Development (post-1st-crack) roasting time 3 minutes, range 1–4. End product temperature 200 °C, range 185–225 °C.
- **Page:** 35.

### Sample-roaster and large-roaster timings differ by a factor of ~3
- **Claim:** Optimum time to 1st crack scales with machine size and heat-transfer mode.
- **Mechanism:** Not explained beyond machine scale.
- **Numbers:** As low as 5 minutes to 1st crack on 100 g sample drum roasters or small convection-based sample roasters; up to 14 minutes on large 300 kg commercial drum roasters. Time from 1st crack to target colour: as little as 1 minute for light roasts, sometimes closer to half a minute for extremely light roasts, up to 4–5 minutes for dark or very dark. Fluid-bed roasters span 50 g (Ikawa) to 600+ kg (Neuhaus Neotec) (p. 25).
- **Evidence:** Assertion from experience.
- **Page:** 36 (and 25).

### The "worst profile" full-flame calibration roast
- **Claim:** On an unfamiliar roaster, deliberately running one full batch at full flame to 2nd crack — the worst possible profile from a quality standpoint — is the fastest way to map the machine's energy potential and the bean temperatures at 1st and 2nd crack.
- **Mechanism:** It bounds the playing field: it reveals the roaster's maximum roast speed and fixes the two waypoints all later profile design hangs on.
- **Evidence:** Personal method, explicitly framed as the author's experience; comes with a fire warning (be ready to terminate; the coffee may catch fire).
- **Numbers:** The author claims he typically hits something close to the rules of thumb after two or three roasts using this method.
- **Page:** 36.

### Repeatability requires a between-roast protocol, not just a profile
- **Claim:** You cannot repeat a roast by repeating the flame sequence alone; the preheating sequence and accumulated between-roast heat are part of the cause and must be standardised. The same preheat is required to get the same result from a given flame control sequence, which demands a formal "between-roast protocol" putting the system in the same state before each batch.
- **Mechanism:** Heat built up in the roaster's metal spills back into the batch and adds energy in parallel with the heat source.
- **Evidence:** Assertion / mechanism.
- **Numbers:** The effect is large in drum roasters (much metal in drum and body) and much smaller — often negligible — in convection systems, because air has low heat capacity and is the only conductor.
- **Page:** 39.

### Roast loss, volume gain and moisture
- **Claim:** Predictable mass and volume changes across the roast, with commercial consequences.
- **Mechanism:** Water boils and vaporises, creating internal pressure; CO₂ takes over as the driving pressure later in the roast; the material expands like rising bread. Since only ~10 percentage points of the 15–20% loss is moisture, the remainder must be organic molecules breaking down into volatile gases, chiefly CO₂.
- **Evidence:** Assertion / standard roasting-science account.
- **Numbers:** Green moisture ~12% at origin; specialty green should arrive at 9–11%; store at ~60% Rh. Roast loss ~15% for light roasts, up to 20% for dark. Roasted moisture 1–1.5%. Volume expansion 50% for light roasts, up to double for dark. Rule of thumb: ~1.2 kg green per 1 kg roasted sold. A 30 kg batch drives off more than three litres of water.
- **Page:** 10, 11, 12.

### Speed of roast changes expansion independently of colour
- **Claim:** A faster roast will have expanded more than a slower roast even if the two end at the same colour.
- **Mechanism:** Rate of pressure development from vapour and CO₂ drives expansion, and that rate depends on roast speed.
- **Evidence:** Assertion.
- **Numbers:** Within the 50–100% expansion range.
- **Page:** 11.

### Colour gradient: colour is a surface reading of an internal distribution
- **Claim:** Colour is not uniform through the bean. Heat travels surface-to-centre, so a temperature — and therefore colour — gradient exists from surface to centre; slow roasts are even from surface to centre, faster/lighter roasts have a steeper gradient, a too-high launch temperature burns the surface of an otherwise well-developed bean, and in extreme industrial flash roasts a burnt surface and a still-green centre can coexist.
- **Mechanism:** Diffusion of heat into the bean takes time; if heat is added faster than it diffuses, the gradient steepens.
- **Evidence:** Assertion, illustrated schematically (Fig. 10). Notably, the book refuses to moralise about it: "a gradient is not necessarily good or bad. The good/bad decision is made at the cupping table and during consumer tests."
- **Numbers:** Flash roast = total time under 3 minutes.
- **Page:** 27, 28.

### Cool fast or the coffee tastes baked
- **Claim:** Coffee must be brought below 30 °C in under five minutes from dumping, or it will taste baked and lose acidity.
- **Mechanism:** Residual heat continues the roast; slow cooling extends it uncontrolled.
- **Evidence:** Stated explicitly as a rule of thumb.
- **Numbers:** <30 °C in <5 minutes. Infrared thermometers recommended for non-contact measurement in the cooling tray.
- **Page:** 18.

### Water quenching: legitimate technique, common adulteration
- **Claim:** Quenching is physically sound (vaporising water absorbs a lot of energy and stops the roast almost instantly) and harmless *if* the water fully vaporises and leaves as steam — but it is frequently used in commodity coffee at a water ratio that deliberately leaves added water sold as coffee.
- **Mechanism:** Endothermic vaporisation drops temperature sharply; excess water raises residual moisture, which speeds degassing and staling.
- **Evidence:** Assertion plus personal observation ("I have, however, seen quenching used with extreme care on high quality beans without a noticeable degradation").
- **Numbers:** Specialty roasted coffee 1–1.5% water; quenched low-quality coffee up to 5% moisture after roasting. Verification method: moisture meters on roasted beans.
- **Page:** 18, 19.

### Air temperature is an early indicator of bean temperature; flame of air
- **Claim:** There is a causal chain flame → air temperature → bean temperature. Flame setting predicts what happens later to air temperature, and air temperature predicts what happens later to bean temperature; the bigger the air-to-bean temperature difference, the faster energy transfers and the faster (and riskier) the roast.
- **Mechanism:** Heat flows from hot to cold on contact; equal temperatures transfer nothing.
- **Evidence:** Physics, asserted.
- **Numbers:** Room air ~20 °C, heated air ~200 °C in the schematic (Fig. 8, p. 24).
- **Page:** 30, 31.

### Convection dominates even in drum roasters
- **Claim:** Even in a flame-on-drum roaster that relies on conduction and radiation, the majority of heat transfer to the beans still comes from hot air.
- **Evidence:** Assertion.
- **Page:** 31.

### Fires are a certainty, not a risk
- **Claim:** "It is not a question of *if* but *when* you will have a fire" — fire routines must exist before the roaster is first switched on. Three fire locations: roasting chamber, ventilation/chimney, cyclone.
- **Mechanism:** Every roast flushes the ventilation with moisture, dry organic dust and coffee oils, building a highly flammable layer.
- **Evidence:** Personal incident reports (a half-batch given a full-batch flame profile caught fire at Kontra, Copenhagen; a chimney glowed red after beans escaped an overfilled drum).
- **Numbers:** Chimney cleaning roughly once every three months. Inert gas system (nitrogen/CO₂/argon) drops chimney oxygen from 21% to under 1% within one minute and keeps feeding for at least 10 minutes. Business risk: lead time on a new roaster is 4 weeks to 4 months, and 80–90% of clients will find another supplier if you cannot deliver after one week.
- **Page:** 42–46 (fire classes table, p. 46).

## Where it cites peer-reviewed research

1. **Giacalone, D., Münchow, M. et al. (2018), "Common roasting defects in coffee: Aroma composition, sensory characterization and consumer perception," *Food Quality and Preference*, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodqual.2018.03.009.** Cited for the central quantitative claim of the book: in a quantitative-descriptive sensory evaluation across a mixture of colour- and time-modulated roast profiles, colour modulated ~80% of the sensory variation and the timing aspect only ~20%. Cited **p. 14**, invoked again without name in the conclusion **p. 48**, listed in Resources **p. 68**. This is the *only* peer-reviewed study cited anywhere in the handbook.

2. **Louis-Camille Maillard, 1912** — cited historically for the first description of the Maillard reaction between small sugars and amino acids from proteins, and to make the point that browning chemistry is not coffee-specific but general food science. **p. 10**. (Attribution only; no data drawn from it.)

Not peer-reviewed but worth logging as its other cited authorities:
- **Heather Ward / SCA, *Roaster-Retailer Financial Benchmarking Report*** — cited as a collection and analysis of key financial indicators for roastery profitability. **p. 51**, **p. 68**.
- **Ken Wilber's Integral Approach** (four-quadrant model) — used as a product/business framework. **p. 59**.
- **E-Myth Mastery** (Michael Gerber), **The Lean Startup**, **Business Model Canvas / Business Model Generation** — the business-half scaffolding. **pp. 57, 63, 65–66, 68**.
- **Rob Hoos, *Modulating the Flavor Profile of Coffee*; Scott Rao, *The Coffee Roaster's Companion*; Roast magazine** — listed as roasting resources without being cited for any specific claim in the text. **p. 68**.
- The author's own ongoing, then-unfinished research project mapping business models of coffee roasteries, presented at the Roasters Guild Camp 2017 and expected to finalise in 2018. **p. 51, 69**.

## What it says is NOT established

- **"Coffee roasting is a dark art."** Explicitly the thing the book exists to refute: it argues roasting is not a mystery reserved for a few, and compares it to baking bread — attend to time and colour, accept some trial and error, and good results follow (pp. 6, 48). The author concedes his own approach "may sometimes lead to oversimplification," preferring clarity over completeness at foundation level (p. 6).
- **That development time = time after 1st crack is physically meaningful.** It says the opposite: the bean develops from the moment it enters the roaster and arguably until it reaches ambient in the cooling tray; the post-1st-crack convention is a traditional focus, defended pragmatically rather than as a fact (pp. 9, 13).
- **That bean temperatures are transferable between machines.** Comparisons of bean-temperature readings across different roasters "can rarely be used" — the number is only valid as a consistent value on the same machine, because probe geometry, position and drum thickness all change both the value and its rate of change (p. 21). End temperature therefore spans 185–225 °C not only because of intended darkness but because of where the probe sits in a given brand (p. 36).
- **That a bean-temperature-to-colour lookup holds across profiles.** It holds only for similar profiles; different profiles have different correlations, and seasonal ambient temperature and humidity shift it too (p. 13).
- **That any of its numbers are targets.** The rules of thumb are "suggested as a useful starting point," designed only to help you avoid scorching (lacking acidity, roasty/bread-crust note) and baking (flat, papery, lacking sweetness); once you hit them your actual profile development begins (pp. 35, 37).
- **That there are universal truths about energy input.** "There are no universal truths but these should be considered as guidelines when you approach a machine that you don't yet know" (p. 39).
- **That a colour gradient is a defect.** It declines to rule: "a gradient is not necessarily good or bad. The good/bad decision is made at the cupping table and during consumer tests" (p. 27). Small gradients suit some coffees and roast styles (p. 28).
- **That any roast degree is right or wrong.** Explicitly refused as a framing; the criterion is the expected customer segment, and a skilled roaster should be able to maximise terroir at any roast degree the segment demands (p. 15).
- **That quenching is inherently adulteration.** It separates the physics (sound) from the common commercial abuse (adding sellable water), and says careful quenching on high-quality beans has not produced noticeable degradation in his experience, verifiable with a moisture meter (pp. 18–19).
- **That anyone can answer "how long should each stage be."** Stated as not easily answerable given the combinations of green coffee, roaster manufacturer and consumer preference (p. 35).

## Gaps relative to what was asked

- **Rate of rise (RoR) is never named, defined, plotted or discussed anywhere in the book.** There is no claim that RoR must decline, no crashing/flick discussion, and no RoR-based diagnostics. The nearest equivalent is the qualitative principle that *energy input* (flame) should be stepped down from high to low across the roast, reduced several times before 1st crack and again after it (pp. 32, 33, 39) — a statement about the control input, not about the derivative of the bean curve. The profile figures (Figs. 11, 12, 13, 16) plot only air and bean temperature against time; no derivative curve appears. Any "RoR must decline" doctrine is *not* in this text.
- **Colour measurement instruments** are acknowledged to exist ("a range of commercial equipment that has been developed to measure this") but no instrument, scale (Agtron, Colorette, etc.), unit, tolerance or measurement protocol is named. The book's explicit position is that you can navigate roast degree *without* expensive colour measurement equipment by using end bean temperature plus 1st and 2nd crack as landmarks (p. 13). So it asserts colour is the dominant sensory variable (p. 14, 48) while giving no method for measuring it — the gap is the book's own.
- **Sample roasting** is not treated as a topic. Sample roasters appear only as size/timing data points: 100 g sample drum roasters and small convection sample roasters can reach 1st crack in 5 minutes (p. 36), and fluid-bed roasters start at 50 g batch size (p. 25). There is no sample-roast protocol, no discussion of whether sample roasts predict production roasts.
- **Measurement error / repeatability** is addressed only through (a) the between-machine probe incomparability argument (p. 21) and (b) the between-roast preheat protocol requirement (p. 39). There are no repeatability statistics, no stated tolerances, no replicate counts, no error bars anywhere in the book.
