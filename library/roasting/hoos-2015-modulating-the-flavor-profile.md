# Modulating the Flavor Profile of Coffee — Rob Hoos

*Modulating the Flavor Profile of Coffee: "One Roaster's Manifesto."* Rob Hoos. Copyright © 2015 by Rob Hoos. Self-published (no publisher imprint stated; "Written and Researched in Portland, OR"; printed in the USA). ISBN 978-0-692-41770-6; Library of Congress Control Number 2015906973. No edition statement. Editing by Kelly Stewart; layout/design by Mark Carter (p. 55). Bibliographic details on p. 2.

**Note on page numbers:** the PDF carries no printed folios, so all page references below are PDF page numbers of the 55-page file.

## What the book is

The book is a practitioner's manifesto that deconstructs the roast curve into four sequential, separately manipulable segments — "drying," "MAI" (colour change to first crack), "development" (first crack to drop), and terminal temperature / degree of caramelization — and asks what happens in the cup when each is lengthened or shortened while the others are held constant (pp. 13, 50–52). The core evidence is a set of blind cupping trials run in the Nossa Familia Coffee lab in Portland on four coffees (a Brazilian micro-lot, a Mexican organic, an SHG Guatemalan, an Ethiopian Kochere), supplemented by logged production roasts and one dedicated terminal-temperature experiment on an SHB Guatemalan (pp. 22–27, 33–37, 41–43). Its central claim is that the flavour profile of a coffee is not black magic but is modulable in predictable *directions* — longer Maillard time buys body and complexity at the cost of clarity; development time steers the organic-acid balance; terminal temperature steers residual sweetness versus caramel/pyrolytic bitterness — while the author is explicit that these are trends and guidelines, not a formal scientific result (pp. 7, 11, 27, 53).

## Findings

### Green coffee sets a ceiling the roast cannot exceed
- **Claim:** Roasting can only reveal or degrade what is chemically present in the green; it cannot manufacture flavours the coffee does not have the potential for.
- **Mechanism:** Intrinsic precursors in the seed determine the available flavour space; the roast selects among and transforms them. The author's analogy: a coffee is a colour — you can shift blue toward teal, navy or purple, but never to orange.
- **Evidence:** Assertion, argued from the author's practice and backed by a quotation from Sivetz, *Coffee Technology* (p. 257). No experiment.
- **Numbers:** None.
- **Page:** 15

### Charge temperature and charge weight set thermal momentum, not flavour
- **Claim:** Charge temperature and batch weight establish the initial thermal momentum of the roast; they do not themselves cause flavour development, though they condition everything downstream.
- **Mechanism:** Charge temperature is a proxy (read off the return/exhaust-air probe) for the heat stored in the drum; it must be adjusted for batch size, batch number in the day, residual heat in the system and the intended heat application. The author states explicitly that charge weight/temperature and turn-around time/temperature "do not directly cause flavor development" but "significantly affect the rest of the roast," and need not be held perfectly constant to reproduce a profile.
- **Evidence:** The author's practice and operating heuristics. No experiment, no cupping data.
- **Numbers:** None given as absolute values. Directional rules only: smaller charge weight → lower charge temperature; heavier charge → higher; later batch in the day → lower charge temperature (thermal retention); a preceding dark roast → lower charge temperature for the next batch.
- **Page:** 16–17

### Turn-around point as an early diagnostic of heat application
- **Claim:** The timing and temperature of the turn-around (the bean probe's RoR crossing zero after its initial drop) tells the roaster whether the system is carrying more or less thermal momentum than planned, long before a meaningful rate of rise is available.
- **Mechanism:** On charge, the probe falls while the bean mass rises; turn-around is where the two meet. Early turn-around (or an abnormally high probe reading) = excess momentum → reduce heat; late turn-around, or a lower-than-normal turn-around temperature = deficit → increase heat.
- **Evidence:** The author's practice; explicitly flagged as "not a precise measurement." Probe type, thickness and placement are said to change the result.
- **Numbers:** Turn-around defined as bean-probe RoR = 0 °F/min, typically after roughly the first minute.
- **Page:** 16–17

### The drying phase contains no real chemical reactions but is where defects are made
- **Claim:** From charge to the onset of colour change to yellow, nothing flavour-forming is happening chemically; the phase matters for defect avoidance and for setting up the rest of the roast.
- **Mechanism:** Free moisture is driven off as steam, pressure begins to build, and thermal momentum is established. Too short a drying phase risks tipping (excessively rapid heat application), scorching (drum surface too hot at charge), facing (already-colour-changed coffee scorched via drum speed, overloading, or excessive conductive transfer), uneven moisture removal, and a "runaway" curve. Too long a drying phase risks insufficient pressure in the bean during later reactions — the author's stated cause of a "flat" coffee — and insufficient momentum to hit the planned curve.
- **Evidence:** The author's practice plus mechanistic reasoning. Explicitly framed as "a control point to help aid in the avoidance of negative flavor contributions," not a positive-flavour lever. The author says the number of variables per situation is too great to cover.
- **Numbers:** None.
- **Page:** 18–19

### Onset of colour change to yellow is the critical measurable landmark
- **Claim:** The temperature and time at which the bean turns yellow (with hay-like aroma) marks the beginning of the Maillard reaction and should be logged and standardized as the roaster's reference point.
- **Mechanism:** Enough moisture has left the bean for non-enzymatic browning to begin; matching this point batch-to-batch produces consistent thermal energy entering the chemical-reaction phases.
- **Evidence:** The author's practice, stated as "absolutely critical" for replication.
- **Numbers:** None (the reader is told to record their own roaster's value).
- **Page:** 17–18

### Longer Maillard (colour change → first crack) increases body and complexity; shorter increases clarity and lightness
- **Claim:** Lengthening the time between colour change and first crack ("MAI") increases perceived body/viscosity and the complexity of sugar-browning descriptors; shortening it lightens the body and increases clarity.
- **Mechanism:** The Maillard reaction continuously generates melanoidins — high-molecular-weight browning products that correlate with higher viscosity and thicker mouthfeel — plus a large volatile pool. More time in the phase = more melanoidins = more body and complexity, at the cost of clarity.
- **Evidence:** **Designed experiment with blind cupping.** Four coffees (Brazil micro-lot, Mexican organic, SHG Guatemalan — run twice, Ethiopian Kochere — cupped twice), all cupped blind in the Nossa Familia lab, with drying time, development time and final temperature held as constant as possible and only MAI varied. Supplemented by a table of production-roast Kochere variances paired with tasting notes. The author reports two outliers against his own hypothesis and declines to explain them away.
- **Numbers:** Control target: all non-varied segments within 0:10 of baseline (the author's stated threshold for tasting a difference). MAI variances actually achieved: Brazil −0:10 / +0:39 (plus an out-of-spec +0:35); Mexican −0:08 / +0:26; Guatemalan round A −0:29 / +0:23 (averaged over 7 cuppings); Guatemalan round B −0:03 / +0:41; Kochere −0:11 / +0:54. Body scores (SCAA qualitative 6–10 | intensity 1–5): Brazil 8|3 → 7.5|2 fast → 8.5|3 slow; Mexican 7.5|3.5 → 7.5|4.5 fast (called an erroneous score) → 8|5 slow; Guatemalan A 7.75|2.75 → 7|2.5 fast → 7.625|3.5 slow; Guatemalan B 7.75|2.75 → 7|2.5 fast → 7.625|3.5 slow (second-round narrative gives baseline 8.5|3.5, fast 8|4, slow 8|2.5 — the outlier the author flags); Kochere 7.5|2 → 8|3.5 fast → 8|3 slow, second cupping 7.5|4.5 → 7.5|2 → 7.5|3. Production-run descriptor table spans −1:12 to +0:53 relative to baseline. The Maillard reaction is credited with "well over 600" volatile organic compounds.
- **Page:** 20–27 (tables 22, 23, 24, 25, 26; production-run table 27; conclusions 27)

### Maillard-time flavour descriptors move along a light→heavy, simple→complex axis
- **Claim:** As MAI lengthens, descriptors migrate from light, unstructured, tea-like/floral terms toward heavy, structured, sugar-browning terms.
- **Mechanism:** Increasing melanoidin load and increasing complexity of the intermediate-product pool.
- **Evidence:** Blind cupping notes from the four experimental coffees plus 13 logged Kochere production runs. Presented as a "clear trend," but descriptor-based rather than scored.
- **Numbers:** The author's worked example of the progression: brown sugar → maple syrup; vanilla → molasses. Kochere production table runs from −1:12 ("cane sugar, honey, vanilla") through 0:00 ("honey-like, vanilla, butterscotch") to +0:53 ("chocolate, malt, caramel, vanilla bean").
- **Page:** 21, 23, 24, 27

### Development time primarily steers the organic-acid balance
- **Claim:** The length of time from first crack to drop is the roaster's main control over acidity — its intensity, its quality, and which acids dominate.
- **Mechanism:** During development, chlorogenic acids (CGAs, held responsible for much of coffee's bitterness) degrade to quinic and caffeic acids; citric and malic acids degrade to citraconic, fumaric and maleic; acetic acid is generated from sucrose caramelization, peaks, then declines; phosphoric acid stays roughly constant (a sourcing issue, not a roasting one). Develop too fast and residual CGAs give bitter/metallic/vegetal acidity; develop too long and acidity is muted, dull or unpleasant.
- **Evidence:** **Designed experiment with blind cupping** on the same four coffees, plus citations to Rivera (CoffeeChemistry.com), Illy & Viani, Sivetz and Flament for the chemistry. The author notes he cannot modulate the post-first-crack Maillard reaction independently of caramelization, acid degradation and pyrolysis, so the segment is confounded by design.
- **Numbers:** Acidity scores (qualitative | intensity), development variance from baseline: Brazil 8.5|3 → 7.5|5 at −0:57 → 6.5|1.5 at +1:03 (this roast also drifted +0:35 in MAI, which the author flags); Mexican 8|4 → 7|4 at −0:35 → 6.75|3 at +0:19; Guatemalan A 8.5|4 → 8.25|3.75 at −0:11 → 6.25|3 at +0:23; Guatemalan B 8.5|4 → 8|4 at −0:43 → 7|3 at +0:12; Kochere 8.5|4 → 7|3.5 at −0:48 → 6.5|4 at +0:46, second cupping 7.5|3.5 → 7.5|4.5 → 7|3. Acetic acid is cited as able to rise up to 25× its green concentration, peaking at light-to-medium roast. A 10-row Kochere production table spans −0:48 to +0:46.
- **Page:** 31–37 (tables 33, 34, 35, 36; production table 37)

### The acidity progression as development lengthens is ordered and monotonic
- **Claim:** Acidity moves through a fixed sequence as development time is extended: bitter → bitter/vegetal → bitter plus pleasant acids → pleasant acids plus sour → pleasant acids plus sweet-and-sour → pleasant acids plus sweet → sweet → dull → bland.
- **Mechanism:** Progressive CGA decomposition (removing bitterness) running ahead of, then overtaking, the decomposition of the desirable citric and malic acids. The optimum is the crossing point.
- **Evidence:** The author's own synthesis of his cupping trials plus production experience; presented as a general rule with an illustrative descriptor ladder ("copper coin" → grapefruit rind → Meyer lemon → melon → low/no acidity). Not itself a separate experiment.
- **Numbers:** None.
- **Page:** 38–40, 51

### Maillard products keep changing after first crack, adding floral/fragrant structure
- **Claim:** Longer development time also increases the structure and complexity of floral and fragrant tones (and of browning tones not formed by caramelization); very fast development leaves those tones unstructured or aggressive and the acidity one-dimensional.
- **Mechanism:** The Maillard reaction continues past first crack but now reacts with new reactants supplied by caramelization, acid degradation and pyrolysis, producing a different product spectrum.
- **Evidence:** Cupping descriptors from the development-time experiments, interpreted by the author; explicitly labelled "my opinion" in places and "a general rule of thumb."
- **Numbers:** None.
- **Page:** 38, 40, 51

### Terminal temperature — not development time — controls sweetness and caramelization
- **Claim:** The degree of sugar caramelization, and therefore how much residual sweetness survives, is governed chiefly by the temperature the batch reaches, largely independently of how long development lasted.
- **Mechanism:** Sugars require added heat, not merely time, to caramelize further (contrasted with organic acids, which the author says respond mostly to time). Lower end temperature = more residual sucrose = sweeter but less complex, with vegetal notes surviving; higher end temperature = more caramels = less sweet, more complex, more bitter, eventually pyrolytic/ashen.
- **Evidence:** **One designed experiment with a SCAA cupping form**, on a single SHB Guatemalan, three roasts (baseline, low drop, high drop), plus the author's accumulated tasting. Single origin, single round, no replication reported.
- **Numbers:** Segment deviations held small — drying 0:00 / +0:03 / +0:10; Maillard 0:00 / +0:03 / +0:03; development 0:00 / +0:03 / +0:08 — with end temperature the only significant variable at 0 °F / −6 °F / +6 °F. SCAA scores: baseline fragrance 8.25, flavour 8.5, aftertaste 8, acidity 8.5, body 8.5, balance 8.5; low drop 7.75 / 7.5 / 8 / 7.5 / 8 / 7.5; high drop 8 / 7.5 / 7.5 / 7.5 / 7 / 7. Uniformity, clean cup and sweetness all 10 across the three.
- **Page:** 41–43

### There is a caramelization "sweet spot," and it is narrow
- **Claim:** Both a lower and a higher drop temperature than the optimum score worse; the desirable range of pyrolysis/caramelization is bounded on both sides.
- **Mechanism:** Too little pyrolysis leaves vegetal contributors intact and un-obscured by caramel tone; too much produces savoury, bitter caramels and eventually carbon/ash, flattening complexity.
- **Evidence:** The single three-roast SHB Guatemalan cupping above. The author words it cautiously — "seem to indicate."
- **Numbers:** A ±6 °F swing in drop temperature produced a full point of movement in several SCAA categories (see previous entry). Descriptors: low drop → garden pea/cucumber, green tea, vegetal peach; high drop → chocolate, sesame seed, syrup, "jagged" body.
- **Page:** 42–43

### Flavour *intensity* tracks the shape of the curve, not the final roast degree
- **Claim:** Changing terminal temperature moves qualitative scores and descriptors but leaves intensity scores essentially unchanged — so intensity is a property of the path the coffee took through the roast, not of how dark it finished.
- **Mechanism:** Not explained beyond the inference itself.
- **Evidence:** The same single terminal-temperature experiment; three roasts, one coffee. Stated as a suggestion ("This would also suggest…").
- **Numbers:** Intensity scores across baseline / low drop / high drop — fragrance 3 / 3 / 3.5; aroma 4 / 3.5 / 3.5; acidity 4 / 4 / 4; body 3.5 / 3.5 / 3.5.
- **Page:** 42–43

### Pressure inside the bean is a first-class flavour variable
- **Claim:** The pressure wall built inside the seed during roasting changes reaction rates and therefore the flavour outcome; it begins forming at charge, before any chemical reaction is underway.
- **Mechanism:** Heat travels inward as a gradient, converting moisture to steam faster than it can escape a still-firm bean, creating an inward-moving pressure front. Higher pressure accelerates gas-phase reactions generally and raises internal temperature — but *suppresses* Maillard volatile formation and retards melanoidin formation specifically.
- **Evidence:** **Assertion plus external literature**, not the author's own experiment. He cites Illy & Viani (*Espresso Coffee*, pp. 180, 184, 198), Flament (*Coffee Flavor Chemistry*, p. 37, reporting Kaufman 1951), and Bristo & Isaacs, "The Effect of High Pressure on the Formation of Volatile Products in a Model Maillard Reaction," *J. Chem. Soc. Perkin Trans. 2* (1999), pp. 2217–2218. He describes the topic as "somewhat new to me."
- **Numbers:** Internal bean pressure cited as reaching up to 25 atm (25 bar / 367 psi), at 170–230 °C over 10–15 minutes.
- **Page:** 46–49

### Fast roasts trade body and complexity for speed — a real tradeoff, not a free lunch
- **Claim:** Faster roasts run at higher internal pressure, which suppresses the melanoidin-forming reactions, so they yield lower body and lower complexity; they can still avoid vegetal/underdeveloped character, but risk a one-dimensional cup.
- **Mechanism:** The pressure-suppression of Maillard volatiles above, combined with pressure-acceleration of the reactions that remove vegetal notes and negative acids. Some reactions speed up while the ones giving body slow down, so time cannot be scaled uniformly.
- **Evidence:** Inference from the cited pressure literature combined with the author's roasting experience. No dedicated cupping experiment on roast speed is presented.
- **Numbers:** None.
- **Page:** 48, 52

### Tipping, scorching and facing are mechanically explained defects
- **Claim:** Three named surface defects have distinct causes and distinct cup signatures.
- **Mechanism:** Tipping = excessively rapid heat application; steam vents through the soft germ tip and chars it (the pressure-wall explanation is given at p. 46). Scorching = drum surface temperature too high at charge; gives toasty, grainy, burnt flavour. Facing = already-colour-changed coffee scorched by drum speed/centrifugal force, overloading, or excessive conductive transfer; gives burnt and char-like character.
- **Evidence:** The author's practice and mechanistic reasoning.
- **Numbers:** None.
- **Page:** 18, 46

### The author's own consistency threshold for matching roasts
- **Claim:** Segment-to-segment variances under 10 seconds are, for this author, below the threshold of tasteable difference; production roasts are targeted within that band.
- **Mechanism:** Stated as a limit of his own palate ("I have trouble tasting variance if it is within 10 seconds").
- **Evidence:** The author's self-assessment, used as the control tolerance for all the cupping experiments in the book.
- **Numbers:** 0:10.
- **Page:** 24

### Ratio/percentage models of the roast are at best a partial account
- **Claim:** Because pressure lets some reactions be compressed in time, breaking a roast into fixed percentages or ratios "may function to a degree" — but the author prefers holding overall roast time similar in order to hold pressure buildup similar, and regards that as the better route to consistency.
- **Mechanism:** Pressure-dependent reaction rates mean the scaling between fast and slow roasts is never uniform; parts of the Maillard reaction actually run *more slowly* in faster roasts, so proportional scaling cannot preserve the cup.
- **Evidence:** Reasoning from the cited pressure literature. No experiment testing ratio-based profiles is presented.
- **Numbers:** None.
- **Page:** 48

### Roast defects are not the only way to get dark, heavy, complex coffee
- **Claim:** Darker-tasting flavours, greater complexity and heavier body can be produced without burning the coffee — i.e. by lengthening Maillard time rather than by pushing terminal temperature.
- **Mechanism:** Body and complexity are melanoidin-driven (time in Maillard), whereas dark/bitter/ashen character is caramelization- and pyrolysis-driven (terminal temperature); the two levers are separable.
- **Evidence:** The author's synthesis of the MAI experiments and the terminal-temperature experiment.
- **Numbers:** None.
- **Page:** 43

## What this source does NOT establish

**The author's own disclaimers.** Hoos states plainly that he is not a scientist, has no gas chromatograph or mass spectrometer, and that the chemistry he offers is his opinion assembled from reading and "drawing of logical conclusions" — he expects to revise it (p. 7). He describes the book as informal by necessity and as "a preamble to the larger, professional, robustly scientific publication that will come at a later date," which he hopes to write with someone who has laboratory access (p. 11). He closes by saying there are "no silver bullets," only "vague guidelines" (p. 53), that "there is no such thing" as the best way to roast (p. 52), and that each coffee's different chemistry means the specifics differ even where the trend holds (p. 27). Several claims are hedged in-text as "in my opinion" (pp. 21, 38, 40) or "seem to indicate" (p. 42).

**Sample sizes are small and unblinded to the author.** The MAI and development-time work rests on four coffees, with the Guatemalan run twice and the Kochere cupped twice; the terminal-temperature work rests on a single coffee and three roasts. Cuppings were blind in the lab (p. 22) but the panel size, number of cuppers, and whether the author was among them are never stated, and no statistics are reported — the "σ from baseline" column in the tables is a time deviation from baseline, not a standard deviation. The Guatemalan A scores are averaged over seven cuppings (p. 24); the rest appear to be single sessions.

**Acknowledged outliers and contaminated roasts.** The Mexican fast-MAI intensity score contradicts the hypothesis and is called "an erroneous score… I am only human," partly excused because the variance was under his own 0:10 threshold (pp. 23–24). The second Guatemalan slow-MAI result is called "an outlier in the true sense of the word," and the author says he is "better off just admitting the flaws in my cupping" while continuing to believe the trend (p. 25). At least two experimental roasts drifted out of specification in a second segment — the Brazil MAI experiment had an out-of-spec roast at +0:35 (pp. 22–23) and the Brazil fast-development roast also varied MAI by +0:35, which he concedes contributed to the descriptors (p. 33).

**Confounding the author concedes he cannot remove.** He states he is unable to modulate the post-first-crack Maillard reaction independently of caramelization, organic-acid degradation and pyrolysis, so "development time" is an inherently multi-variable segment (pp. 38, 31). He also notes that the drying phase's effect is indirect and that the number of variables governing charge temperature, turn-around and heat application in any given shop is "too great for me to cover" (p. 19).

**No evidence of its own for the pressure argument.** The pressure chapter — which underwrites the fast-roast tradeoff and the scepticism about ratio-based profiling — rests entirely on cited literature (Illy & Viani; Flament; Bristo & Isaacs 1999) plus reasoning. No roast experiment or cupping in the book tests roast speed or pressure directly, and the author says the topic is "somewhat new to me" (p. 46). The one in-coffee number offered (25 atm) is Illy's, not measured here.

**Subjectivity of the flavour vocabulary is explicit.** Appendix A states that the descriptors are records of the author's personal experience of taste — "lemon to lime" means he perceived more bittering, not a compositional claim — and advises readers to think in terms of their own experience of taste rather than the chemistry (p. 54).

**Topics the book does not address at all.** Despite being a book about roast-curve shape, it contains no treatment of rate-of-rise *shape* as a flavour variable, no discussion of the RoR "crash" or "flick," and no development time ratio (DTR) percentage target — the nearest thing is the passing scepticism about percentage/ratio breakdowns at p. 48. Batch size is mentioned only as an input to charge temperature and thermal momentum (pp. 16–17), never varied experimentally. First crack is treated purely as the boundary marker between the MAI and development segments (pp. 21, 31, 50); its own timing, intensity or relation to bean moisture is not studied. Nothing is labelled a "myth" in so many words; the closest explicit rejections are that flavour compounds are not "black magic" (p. 12), that roasting cannot add potential that is not in the green (p. 15), that sweetness/caramelization is *not* mainly a function of development-time length (p. 41), and that intensity is *not* a function of final roast degree (p. 43).
