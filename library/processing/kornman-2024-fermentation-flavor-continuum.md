**Citation convention.** This document carries printed page numbers. Below I cite **printed page** with the PDF page in brackets, e.g. `p.11 [PDF 13]`. The offset is printed = PDF − 2.

**What this document is, commercially.** It is a marketing-adjacent educational booklet published by a green coffee importer whose own tasting lab (The Crown) sells classes and cuppings. That does not make it wrong — Kornman is careful and repeatedly flags uncertainty — but it is trade literature, not a peer-reviewed source, and it names no studies.

---

## What it is

A short, plain-language primer for green buyers and roasters on how coffee fermentation works and how each processing style tends to land on the cupping table. Its scope is **primary processing only** — from harvest through drying — and it deliberately stops before roasting; the roast is named once, in the preface, as one of the "myriad factors" alongside cultivar and brewing that the booklet is *not* about. It rests on the author's professional cupping and green-grading experience at an importer, on trade convention, and on repeated but entirely unattributed appeals to "researchers", "recent studies" and "certain scientists"; the one concrete source it names is the author's own earlier book, from which the processing-types infographic is reprinted.

## Process reference

There is no roast-level table in this document and no roasting temperatures anywhere in it — it is a green-side document. The two reference tables it does carry are reproduced below.

### Figure 1 — "Coffee Processing Types" matrix (p.9 [PDF 11])

The infographic is reprinted from Kornman's *Green Coffee: A Guide for Roasters & Buyers* (© Chris Kornman 2022), designed by Chris Kornman, Evan Gilman and Jeremy Leff. It cross-tabulates **9 processing types** against **10 processing steps**, with each cell coded on a 5-level frequency key: **Always / Usually / Sometimes / Rarely / Partially**.

Processing types (columns): Fully Washed · Double Washed · Triple Washed · Natural (Dried in Cherry) · Pulped Natural / Honey · Wet Hulled · Anaerobic · Carbonic Maceration · Eco-Pulped / Demucilaged

Processing steps (rows): Cherry Flotation · Depulping · Fermentation/Maceration (traditional) · Fermentation/Maceration (sealed tank) · Washing Channels (scrubbing & grading) · Post-Fermentation Soak · Dried in Cherry · Dried in Clean Parchment · Dried in Parchment with Mucilage · Dried as Seed (green bean)

The structurally important readings from the matrix:

| Processing type | Depulped? | Traditional (open) ferment | Sealed-tank ferment | Post-ferment soak | Dried as |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fully Washed | always | always | never | sometimes | clean parchment |
| Double Washed | always | always | never | always | clean parchment |
| Triple Washed | always | always | never | always | clean parchment |
| Natural (dried in cherry) | never | never (the cherry is simply dried) | never | never | whole cherry |
| Pulped Natural / Honey | always | rarely (spontaneous, not intentional) | never | never | parchment with mucilage |
| Wet Hulled | always | **usually — this is the row that distinguishes it** | never | never | **as bare seed / green bean** — the only column with this |
| Anaerobic | usually but not always | never | always | usually | in cherry or in parchment w/ mucilage |
| Carbonic Maceration | only *after* the ferment, if at all | never | always, **whole cherry** | usually | in cherry or in parchment w/ mucilage |
| Eco-Pulped / Demucilaged | always | rarely | never | rarely | clean parchment |

Read as a diagnostic: **the axis that actually separates the categories is not "washed vs natural" but four independent binary-ish choices** — (1) is the fruit removed before fermenting, (2) is the ferment open or sealed, (3) is there a post-ferment soak, (4) is the coffee dried in cherry, in mucilage-coated parchment, in clean parchment, or as bare seed. Every named process is a combination of those four.

### Figure 2 — "Mapping The Flavor Wheel to Process" (p.13 [PDF 15])

A three-column Sankey diagram with the axes labelled, left to right, **Flavor → Category → Process**. This is the closest the document comes to defining "the continuum" as a measurable object.

- **Flavor** (individual descriptors, 25 of them): Blackberry, Raspberry, Blueberry, Strawberry, Raisin, Prune, Coconut, Cherry, Pomegranate, Pineapple, Grape, Apple, Peach, Pear, Grapefruit, Orange, Lemon, Lime, Acetic Acid, Citric Acid, Malic Acid, Winey, Fermented, Whiskey, Overripe.
- **Category** (6 groupings): Berry · Dried Fruit · Other Fruit · Citrus Fruit · Sour · Alcohol/Fermented.
- **Process** (3 terminals only): **Traditional Natural · Traditional Washed · Fermentation Defect.**

The flows as drawn:

| Category | Flavors feeding it | Flows predominantly to |
|---|---|---|
| Berry | Blackberry, Raspberry, Blueberry, Strawberry | Traditional Natural |
| Dried Fruit | Raisin, Prune, (Coconut, Cherry) | Traditional Natural |
| Other Fruit | Cherry, Pomegranate, Pineapple, Grape, Apple, Peach, Pear | split — Natural and Washed both |
| Citrus Fruit | Grapefruit, Orange, Lemon, Lime | Traditional Washed |
| Sour | Acetic acid, Citric acid, Malic acid | split — Washed, and a strand into Fermentation Defect (acetic) |
| Alcohol/Fermented | Winey, Fermented, Whiskey, Overripe | Fermentation Defect, with a strand back into Traditional Natural |

Three things this figure establishes that matter for a diagnostics dictionary. First, **the defect terminal is not separate from the process terminals — flavors flow into it from the same categories**, meaning the same descriptor can be a virtue or a fault depending on intensity and consistency. Second, **"Winey" sits in the Alcohol/Fermented category and flows to both Traditional Natural and Fermentation Defect**, which is precisely the ambiguity the whole booklet is about. Third, **acetic acid is the sour that leans toward defect** while citric and malic lean toward washed quality.

**Important limit on Figure 2:** its Process axis has only three terminals. The entire "New Frontiers" half of the booklet — honey, multi-stage, anaerobic, carbonic, co-fermented, inoculated — **is not on this diagram at all**. The mapping is explicitly the *traditional* two-process world plus the defect, and Kornman's core argument is that the new processes have broken it.

## Findings

### The "continuum" is a spectrum from clean to over-fermented, and the quality line on it has moved

- **Claim:** Coffee flavour arising from fermentation lies on a single spectrum running from mild/clean at one end through fruity to boozy/winey/vinegary at the far end. There is no natural boundary on this spectrum between "excellent" and "defective" — the boundary is a moving convention.
- **Mechanism:** Increasing microbial activity on the fruit (and, at the extreme, on the seed itself) produces increasingly intense fruit, then alcohol and acid, then rot-adjacent flavours. Where a coffee lands is a function of how much fermentation was allowed and under what control.
- **Evidence:** **Author's assertion, framed as a change in industry consensus.** He states plainly that flavours which "fifteen or twenty years ago would have been rejected as 'overly fermented'" are now accepted. No study, no data, no survey.
- **Numbers / criteria:** The acceptance test he offers is explicitly **two-part and non-sensory**: if the coffee tastes boozy and winey **(a) without cup variation** and **(b) was crafted with intent**, it is accepted as successful — "even if we might individually find it unpalatable". Reference point given for the mild end: "container-loads of generic **82 point** washed coffees tasting like caramel, cacao, nuts, and only the vaguest of fruits."
- **Page:** p.2 [PDF 4].
- **Why this matters for a roast coach:** the two acceptance criteria are *cup uniformity* and *producer intent* — neither of which is a flavour descriptor. A tool that classifies a coffee only by tasting notes cannot distinguish a successful anaerobic from a ferment defect. It needs the process metadata.

### Definition of fermentation — two definitions, and the booklet uses the loose one

- **Claim:** "Fermentation" means something stricter to biochemists than to food producers, and coffee uses the loose sense.
- **Mechanism:** Strict/chemical definition given as: metabolically and enzymatically induced energy extraction from carbohydrates **in the absence of oxygen**. Food-and-beverage definition given as: microbes (usually bacteria or yeasts) bringing about changes in what we eat and drink, typically consuming sugars and producing alcohol and organic acids.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion / standard textbook definitions restated. Uncited.
- **Note:** He flags his own inconsistency implicitly — acetic acid bacteria, which he lists as a fermentation microbe, are **aerobes**, so they do not fit the strict anaerobic definition. This matters because it means "anaerobic processing" is not a redundancy: it genuinely excludes a microbial group.
- **Page:** pp.4–5 [PDF 6–7].

### The microbial cast, and what each group tastes like

- **Claim:** Fermentation microbes divide into bacteria (acetic acid bacteria and lactic acid bacteria) and fungi (chiefly *Saccharomyces* yeasts), each with a characteristic sensory signature.
- **Mechanism / mappings:**
  - **Acetic acid bacteria** — **aerobes**, thrive with oxygen. Produce vinegar; a component of kombucha SCOBYs. Perceived as **sour**.
  - **Lactic acid bacteria** — **anaerobic**. Agents of yogurt, cheese, kimchi, sauerkraut, some beers and wines. Flavours run a wide gamut: lightly sour or tart, often salty (salt brine favours them by making the environment hostile to competitors).
  - **Yeasts / fungi** — *Saccharomyces* is the most common genus in food and beverage; responsible for most wine, beer and bread, and frequently found in mixed-culture ferments. Other fungal genera give savoury products: miso, tempeh, soy sauce.
- **Evidence:** Standard food-science material, restated without citation. Uncontroversial.
- **Page:** p.6 [PDF 8].
- **Practical read:** if a coffee is marketed as "lacto-fermented", the anaerobic LAB route predicts tart/lightly sour rather than the sharp vinegar note of an acetic ferment. That inference is the author's framework applied, not something he states directly.

### Essentially all coffee is fermented

- **Claim:** Asked "is coffee fermented?", the answer is "almost always yes" — fermentation begins immediately after harvest, before any deliberate processing step.
- **Mechanism:** Coffee fruits begin fermenting as soon as they are picked, prior to further processing and export.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion, stated flatly and treated as settled trade knowledge.
- **Page:** p.8 [PDF 10].
- **Consequence he draws:** "washed vs natural" is not fermented-vs-unfermented; both are fermented, differently.

### Traditional natural: spontaneous, uncontrolled, and barely studied

- **Claim:** Natural processing dries the whole fruit around the seed with minimal intervention, and the fermentation that occurs is spontaneous, uncontrolled and generally unobserved.
- **Mechanism:** Microbes native to the growing environment feed on the fruit as it dries.
- **Evidence:** **Explicit statement of an evidence gap** — "very little research has been done into the specific types of microbes responsible for natural fermentation." He offers only a confidence claim: "we can be fairly certain it will be a mixed culture of bacteria and yeast, with specific species likely varying by region." This is the most honest passage in the booklet.
- **Flavour mapping:** Baseline expectation is **fruity**, ranging from "delicately peachy" to "bombastically berry-like". The failure modes at either end that cuppers avoid: **overly nutty and bland** on one side, **winey, boozy, vinegar-like, or composty** on the other.
- **Page:** p.8 [PDF 10].

### Traditional washed: depulped then fermented, and the controls are minimal

- **Claim:** Washed coffee is depulped, fermented "semi-formally", then washed. The fermentation is intentional but the culture is not controlled.
- **Mechanism:** Fermentation in an uncovered tank, either piled dry or submerged in water. Traditionally categorised by researchers as a **mixed culture of predominantly lactic acid bacteria and yeasts**, but the culture will still be shaped by spontaneously occurring native microbes.
- **Evidence:** "Researchers and scientists have traditionally categorized this as..." — **appeal to research, no researcher, paper or year named.** The rest is trade convention.
- **Numbers:** Duration is "usually brief, **eight to 48 hours** may be the most common length, depending on regional trends and temperatures". Monitoring of temperature, sugar content and pH is stated to be *possible*, but traditional controls are limited in practice to **two levers only**: duration, and whether the depulped coffee is submerged. Wet fermentation is said to homogenise the process and "may limit the fermenting microbe population to more beneficial types."
- **Flavour mapping:** Washed coffees are the majority of world specialty production and are expected to taste **clean, mild, sweet**. Regional stereotypes given: generic Central American washed → **caramelly and nutty**; African washed → **more citric and floral**. Crucially: washed coffees "may taste fruity but rarely dive deeply into berry-like flavors, tasting closer to **stone fruit and citrus**".
- **Page:** p.11 [PDF 13].

### The ferment defect — and the argument that it should be redefined chemically

- **Claim:** "Ferment" is a recognised sensory defect that cuppers are trained to detect; it can affect any processing style but is most associated with washed coffees.
- **Presentation / how it shows:** **Typically inconsistent cups** — that is its diagnostic signature — though in severe cases whole lots are affected. Sensory profile: **overly fruity tasting notes, or flavors of vinegar, rotting fruit, or compost.**
- **Causes given:** (1) too long in the fermentation tank; (2) insufficiently cleaned fermentation facilities; (3) maceration by "bad" microbial actors.
- **Mechanism — the substantive claim:** recent work indicates the ferment defect may be a microbial **"attack on the bean" itself**, rather than fermentation of the fruit surrounding the seed. This is a mechanistic distinction with real diagnostic weight: it says the defect is a different physical event, not just more of the same process.
- **Evidence:** "**Recent studies indicate**..." and "**Certain scientists have proposed**..." — **two appeals to unnamed research.** No paper, author, journal or year. This is the weakest evidentiary point in the document relative to how load-bearing the claim is.
- **Proposed marker:** a **chemical rather than sensory** definition of the defect, based on **a threshold of detection of certain ethyl esters** responsible for the off-flavours. No specific ester, no threshold value, no analytical method given.
- **Page:** pp.11–12 [PDF 13–14].
- **For a diagnostics dictionary:** the single most useful operational test here is **cup inconsistency**. Intense fruit that is *uniform across cups* points to intentional processing; the same intensity *varying cup to cup* points to the defect.

### Honey process is itself a continuum, colour-coded by residual mucilage

- **Claim:** Honey / pulped natural / semi-washed is an intermediate: depulp, then dry in the mucilage **without intentionally fermenting**. The spontaneous fermentation resembles a natural's, except that only the pulp clinging to the seed ferments, not a whole cherry.
- **The scale (least to most fruit remaining):** **white → yellow → red → purple → black honey.** White honey leaves almost no fruit on the seed; black honey leaves all but the skin.
- **Flavour mapping:** White honey may be **almost indistinguishable from traditional washed**. Black honey **echoes traditional naturals — usually raisins and grapes**. Yellow and red honeys have a **"pulpy" ripe cherry or plum character**, without being as overtly fruity as the darker grades.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion / trade convention. Historical claim: the first honey process coffees were created in **Brazil in the early 1990s** and eventually made available in small volumes to roasters. No source for the date.
- **Page:** pp.14–15 [PDF 16–17].
- **This is the clearest worked example of the booklet's thesis** — a named process that is not a category but a dial, with a defined scale and a predicted flavour gradient along it.

### Multi-stage fermentation: the post-ferment soak is normal, not exotic

- **Claim:** Extra fermentation stages are used to shape flavour, and one of them — the post-fermentation soak — is already standard practice in a major producing region.
- **Mechanisms and mappings:**
  - **Post-fermentation soak / "double washed"** — commonly employed in most **specialty-grade washed East African** coffees. After the parchment is fermented and washed, it is held submerged in clean water overnight or for a day or two. **"Scientists have confirmed that there is still microbial activity during this stage"**, so it is a slowed secondary fermentation. Predicted result: **extra clean tasting, or especially complex acidities.**
  - **"Double fermented"** — a ferment or maceration in whole cherry *before* depulping and a second ferment in parchment; or a dry ferment followed by a submerged stage. Predicted result: **increased complexity of sweetness, slightly fruitier than traditional washed.**
  - **"Fermented (or macerated) naturals" / "winey process"** — cherries ripen in a **sealed bag or on a raised bed under tarps** before drying. Named "winey" because the flavours resemble "the bigger, boozier characteristics of a California cabernet."
- **Evidence:** Trade convention plus one more unnamed appeal to research ("scientists have confirmed"). Named producer example elsewhere in the booklet: **Edwin Noreña**, Colombia, whose fermentation tanks, barrels and lab are photographed (p.16 [PDF 18]).
- **Page:** p.15 [PDF 17].

### Anaerobic and carbonic maceration: the unifying variable is the sealed vessel, not the flavour

- **Claim:** The only thing all "anaerobic" coffees share is that the producer used a controlled, sealed environment. The term guarantees nothing about the resulting cup.
- **Mechanism:** Sealing off the coffee, usually in stainless steel fermentation tanks fitted with a **one-way valve for off-gassing**. Excluding oxygen gives control over the microbe population.
- **Definitional distinction he draws:**
  - **Carbonic maceration** — term lifted directly from wine; in coffee it typically indicates **whole-cherry fermentation in a sealed tank**, usually dried with some or all of the fruit still on the seed afterwards.
  - **Anaerobic** — applied broadly to **any** sealed-tank ferment, and "offers no clarity as to whether the coffee might resemble standard processing or be something truly unique". Coffee is often depulped before anaerobic fermentation, "but not always".
- **Flavour mapping:** Typically **over-the-top fruitiness and artificial sweetness — bubble gum, fruit candy, sweet dessert wines.**
- **The important caveat, and it is his:** anaerobic technique is increasingly applied to otherwise traditional washed coffees, simply replacing the ad hoc ferment with a controlled one. **"In some cases, the coffee may not even taste much different, and might not even be called 'anaerobic' despite the extra processing details."** So the label under-reports and over-promises in both directions.
- **Duration effect:** because sealed environments give control over the microbe population, **fermentation duration is commonly extended well beyond traditional timeframes**, and the sealed tank **mitigates the over-ferment defect risk** that open uncontrolled environments carry. No duration figures are given.
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion from professional observation. No study, no numbers.
- **Page:** p.17 [PDF 19].

### Co-fermented / "infused" coffees: real enzymatic flavour transfer, not post-roast flavouring

- **Claim:** Coffees fermented together with additional ingredients — fruits, spices, other foods — acquire those flavours through the fermentation itself, not through added flavouring.
- **Mechanism:** **"Enzymatic transfer of flavors occur when microbes are actively macerating more than one substance together"**, so strawberry, cinnamon or even chili-pepper flavours can appear in the resulting coffee "without using traditional post-roasting 'artificial flavoring' methods".
- **Evidence:** Author's assertion. The enzymatic-transfer mechanism is stated without citation and is the single strongest chemical claim in the booklet with the least support behind it. He acknowledges the practice is "controversial in some circles".
- **Page:** pp.17–18 [PDF 19–20].

### Inoculated coffees: predictable, but not proven better

- **Claim:** Deliberate use of a specific bacterial or fungal starter culture to control the fermentation population. Rare in coffee, standard in winemaking.
- **Mechanism:** A starter culture controls the microbe population's exclusivity, giving a stable and predictable ferment.
- **Benefits claimed:** reduced risk of safety concerns like **molds**, and reduced risk of quality concerns like **the ferment defect**.
- **Evidence and the caveat that matters:** **"Claims of increased cup score, however, remain verifiable only on a case-by-case basis and inoculation should not necessarily be taken as a guarantee of better tasting coffee than traditional native-microbe fermentation."** This is an explicit refusal to endorse a marketing claim, and it is the most useful sentence in the section.
- **Real-world examples named:** existing **champagne and other wine yeasts**; the **Catracha project** using a **kombucha SCOBY** for inoculation; coffees designated **"Lacto-fermented"**. Yeast suppliers are said to be actively developing coffee-specific strains, but none are named.
- **Comparison drawn:** in at-scale winemaking most fermentations are inoculated with yeasts developed to highlight recognisable varietal flavours (Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio) — coffee has no equivalent yet.
- **Page:** p.18 [PDF 20].

### Where processing is heading, and why

- **Claim:** Two opposing pressures. Climate-driven water scarcity will push commercial-scale producers toward water-conserving processing; simultaneously, a creative period is pushing producers toward flavour-driven experimentation.
- **Mechanism:** Drought and unpredictable water supply make water-hungry washed processing untenable in some regions; the alternatives named are **honey processing, dry fermenting for washed coffees, and ecopulping**. Changing processing changes fermentation, and therefore flavour.
- **Evidence:** Author's forecast. Explicitly speculative — the closing section is framed as open questions ("The answers to these questions are only beginning to be written").
- **Page:** p.20 [PDF 22].
- **Practical consequence for a roaster's records:** a coffee from a given origin may change its processing style between crops for reasons of water availability, independent of any flavour intent. Processing metadata needs to be recorded per lot, not inferred from origin.

## How a roaster should treat differently-processed coffee differently

**This is the honest answer: the document does not say.** It contains no roasting guidance at all — no temperatures, no times, no development targets, no charge-temperature advice, no drying-phase adjustment for high-mucilage coffees. The preface names roast degree as one of the factors it is *not* addressing. Nothing below is a roasting instruction from Kornman; what follows is what the document actually gives a roaster, stated as such.

1. **Expect a different flavour baseline before you roast, and set the roast target against it.** The mapping the document supports: washed → clean, mild, sweet, caramel/nutty (Central America) or citric/floral (Africa), stone fruit and citrus rather than berry. Natural → fruity, peach through berry. Honey → graded by colour, from washed-like (white) to natural-like raisin/grape (black), with pulpy cherry/plum in the middle. Anaerobic/carbonic → intense, candy-like, bubble gum and dessert wine. Co-fermented → the co-ferment's own flavour, genuinely present in the seed. (pp.8, 11, 15, 17 [PDF 10, 13, 17, 19])

2. **Do not read a process label as a guarantee.** "Anaerobic" is explicitly stated to carry no information about whether the coffee will taste unusual, and some anaerobically fermented coffees are not labelled as such. Honey without a colour grade is under-specified. "Inoculated" is not evidence of higher quality. (pp.17, 18 [PDF 19, 20])

3. **Use cup uniformity, not intensity, to separate a successful ferment from the ferment defect.** The defect's signature is **inconsistency across cups**; intense fruit that is uniform and was made intentionally is, by the booklet's own stated criterion, a success. This is the one genuinely operational diagnostic rule in the document. (pp.2, 12 [PDF 4, 14])

4. **Treat "winey", "boozy", "vinegar", "overripe" and "composty" as ambiguous, not as automatic faults.** Figure 2 draws Alcohol/Fermented flowing to both Traditional Natural and Fermentation Defect. A diagnostics dictionary that hard-codes these as defects will misclassify successful naturals and macerated coffees. (p.13 [PDF 15])

5. **Distinguish acetic sourness from citric/malic sourness.** In Figure 2, acetic acid is the sour that leans toward the defect terminal; citric and malic lean toward washed quality. (p.13 [PDF 15])

6. **Record the four underlying process variables, not just the process name.** Figure 1 shows that the named categories are combinations of: fruit removed before fermenting or not; ferment open or sealed; post-ferment soak or not; dried in cherry / in mucilage-coated parchment / in clean parchment / as bare seed. Two coffees labelled differently can share three of four; two labelled the same can differ. Wet hulled is the only style dried **as bare green seed**, which is a genuinely distinct physical state entering the roaster. (p.9 [PDF 11])

7. **Expect process to change between crops of the same coffee.** Water scarcity is named as a driver pushing producers toward honey, dry-fermented washed, and ecopulping. (p.20 [PDF 22])

## What it does not establish

1. **No roasting guidance whatsoever.** No temperatures, times, curves, development targets, charge adjustments, or drying-phase advice for any processing style. Anyone using this to justify "roast naturals differently" will find the conclusion but not the instruction.

2. **Not one study is named.** The document leans on research four separate times — "researchers and scientists have traditionally categorized", "recent studies indicate", "certain scientists have proposed", "scientists have confirmed" — and names no author, paper, journal, institution or year in any of them. The only named source in the entire document is the author's own prior book, and that is cited for a reprinted infographic, not for a finding.

3. **The ferment-defect chemistry is a pointer, not a marker.** The proposal to define the defect chemically via "certain ethyl esters" gives no ester, no threshold concentration, no analytical method and no citation. It cannot be implemented or verified from what is here.

4. **No quantitative measurements anywhere.** The only numbers in the document are the washed fermentation duration (8–48 hours) and one illustrative cup score (82 points). There is no pH, no Brix, no temperature, no microbial count, no duration for anaerobic or carbonic ferments, no drying time, no moisture target.

5. **The flavour-to-process mapping is a hand-drawn expectation, not a dataset.** Figure 2 is a Sankey diagram with no stated underlying data — no cupping sample size, no scoring protocol, no method for how flow widths were assigned. It reads as an expert's schematic of trade expectations, and should be used as a hypothesis generator rather than as evidence.

6. **Figure 2 covers only three process terminals.** Traditional natural, traditional washed, and the defect. Honey, multi-stage, anaerobic, carbonic, co-fermented and inoculated coffees — the entire second half of the booklet — appear nowhere on the flavour map. So the document's central argument (that new processes have scrambled the old flavour-to-process inference) is asserted in prose but not represented in the one figure that could have shown it.

7. **No treatment of drying as distinct from fermentation.** Drying rate, bed depth, moisture endpoint, and water activity — all of which bear heavily on how a coffee roasts — are outside scope. Figure 1 records only *what the coffee is dried in*, not how.

8. **No cultivar interaction.** Variety is named in the preface as one of the "myriad factors" and then dropped. The booklet does not address whether processing effects differ by cultivar.

9. **Publisher interest.** Published by a green coffee importer whose education arm sells classes and cuppings, and reprinting a figure from the author's own book. Kornman is notably restrained — he declines to endorse inoculation's quality claims and repeatedly flags uncertainty — but this is trade literature and should be weighted as an expert practitioner's synthesis, not as a research review.
